## Summary
The paper asks whether a ledger of concluded A/B tests carries out-of-time predictive skill about the next test, and builds Moot Court, a ladder of predictors (cell base rate, empirical-Bayes Student-t prior, guarded kernel-weighted precedents) that issues a frozen pre-launch card with abstention. It evaluates the ladder with a rolling-origin backtest on the Upworthy exploratory split (13,247 scored contrasts, 3,797 scored tests, nine quarterly origins) and the ASOS dataset (337 scored contrasts, 68 experiments), with proper scores, floors and ceilings, calibration by precision stratum, gates from a power simulation, and decision value kept apart from calibration. On Upworthy the prior improves the logarithmic score over the base-rate rung by 0.145 nats and covers at nominal rates (backtest-upworthy tables.aggregate cell=ALL: P1 logscore -0.8812 against P0 -1.0259, coverage_80 0.8122, coverage_90 0.8945), but its Brier skill against the cell base rate is 0.0015 (-0.0018, 0.0048), the precedent rung is held at the prior in 92.6 percent of cell-origin pairs (p2-guard-upworthy guarded.share_lambda_zero 0.9259), every learned challenger scores at or below the prior, and ranking by predicted win probability captures 0.2489 (P1) and 0.2558 (P2) of realized positive lift against 0.2498 for random order and 0.6824 for an oracle. A first run that scored relative lift at realized precision produced a reversed calibration gradient and a Brier skill of -0.107, which the paper diagnoses as leakage through the reference arm and the realized standard error and turns into a protocol rule. The contribution offered is the protocol, that leakage finding, a powered negative result on precedent-based skill, and a reproducible public benchmark; ASOS returns undecidable at every gate as designed, and six pre-registered hypotheses are reported as they came out (one confirmed, two in part, three refuted).

## Findings

- [blocking] Section 4.2 (P0), Table 1, abstract ("each a strict generalization of the one below"). The text says "P0 is the baseline every skill number is measured against", yet Table 1 reports P0 with a Brier skill of -0.124 (backtest-upworthy tables.aggregate cell=ALL rung=P0 brier_skill -0.1237, ece 0.1016 at the 100th percentile) against "the base rate", and the body never explains how the base rate can score -0.124 against itself. In the code (src/mootcourt/predictors/p0.py, harness.py line 659) the skill denominator is the cell's out-of-time empirical significant-positive frequency (p_base), while the P0 row's p_sig comes from the Normal(mean(y), var(y)) predictive convolved again with s_star, which double-counts measurement noise and is badly miscalibrated. So the scored P0 is not the baseline, the baseline forecaster is never scored as a rung, and P1 does not nest P0 (P1 deconvolves measurement error and fits by marginal likelihood; no setting of nu or tau reproduces the scored P0, whereas lambda = 0 does reproduce P1 exactly). What would fix it: score the empirical-frequency forecaster as P0 (its Brier skill is then zero by construction and its calibration is a real out-of-time check), keep the normal-of-y predictive only as a direction and interval baseline if at all, explain the -0.124 row or remove it, and restrict "strict generalization" to the P1 to P2 step (or make P1 formally nest the new P0 in its normal limit). Every sentence that says "against the base rate" should say which base rate.

- [major] Abstract, Section 1 and Section 6.4 (decision value). For a rung without features, p_win = P(theta > delta) under the cell prior is the same number for every test in a cell at an origin; decision_value.py then breaks ties at the cut by their expected value under a random tie-break, so the P1 policy is "choose by cell, then at random". Its capture of 0.2489 against 0.2498 for random (tables.decision_value origin=ALL) is therefore a property of the rung's design, not a finding about the ledger, and the abstract presents it as the latter ("ranking tests by predicted win probability captures the same share of realized positive lift as random order"). The rungs that could rank within a cell (P2 where lambda > 0, text_lr, local_eb, GBM) are the ones whose decision value answers the question, and only P2 is reported. What would fix it: state in the abstract and Section 6.4 that P1's decision value equals random order by construction except for between-cell differences (the synthetic result in Appendix B already shows this), add decision-value rows for the three challengers and for the unguarded P2 on the same periods, and report a bootstrap-by-period interval for each policy's capture minus random (the text concedes "no interval is computed for the difference from random order"; H5 cannot be called refuted without one).

- [major] Title, abstract ("the forecasts are calibrated and carry no discrimination"), Section 6.3, Section 9. The expected calibration error of P1 and P2 sits at the 100th percentile of the perfect-calibration null (tables.aggregate cell=ALL rung=P1 ece 0.0137 ece_percentile 100.0; rung=P2 ece 0.0219 ece_percentile 100.0): the paper's own test rejects calibration of the issued probabilities, and H3 is recorded as held only in part. Coverage of the intervals for y is at nominal (0.8122 and 0.8945). "Calibrated" in the title and abstract is therefore true of the intervals and false of the probabilities as tested. What would fix it: say "coverage-calibrated, with a small one-sided overconfidence of the event probability (ECE 0.014) that the perfect-calibration null rejects at this n", in the abstract and in the one place the title is justified; consider dropping "Calibrated" from the title or qualifying it.

- [major] Abstract and Section 1 ("a protocol in which nothing measured during a test can reach a forecast made before it") against Section 9 ("two post-launch quantities used as pre-launch stand-ins", a measured correlation of 0.10 between log impressions and the absolute standardized effect; this number is one of the four marked PENDING and must land with a report path). The absolute sentence is contradicted by the limitations paragraph, and a reviewer who reaches Section 9 will not forgive the abstract. What would fix it: qualify the sentence everywhere it appears ("nothing measured during a test reaches a forecast except two stated stand-ins the archive forces, whose leak is measured"), and add the sensitivity run the paragraph itself names: define the significance event on the planned s_star as well and report the main table under both event definitions. That run is one flag and would make the clean protocol fully pre-launch for the readers who need it to be.

- [major] Section 5 (Gates), Section 6.2, Section 9. The gate sizes come from a simulation at base rate 0.3 (gates.json base_rate 0.3; 0.90 power: n_200 175, n_400 375), while the archive's event rate is near 0.09 (tables.reliability_bins rung=P1: 6,590 rows at mean_p 0.0786 with freq 0.0789, 6,508 rows at mean_p 0.1281 with freq 0.1028). The Brier of a base-rate forecaster at 0.09 is about 0.082 against 0.21 at 0.3, so a skill of 0.10 is a far smaller absolute Brier difference and the rows needed to resolve it are not the ones the simulation reports. "A powered negative result" is then supported by the interval (-0.0018, 0.0048), which excludes 0.10 by a wide margin, and not by the gate as computed. What would fix it: re-run `moot simulate power --base-rate 0.1` (the flag exists in cli.py line 1009), report that gate beside the 0.3 one, and let the text say the negative is powered because the interval excludes the target, with the gate as the sizing convention it is.

- [major] Section 2 ("what appears new") and Appendix C. The authors' own landscape survey (docs/landscape.md, sections 3.7, 3.8, 3.11, 3.15 and the gap table) describes three 2026 industry systems that screen candidates before a live test: an archive-trained creative ranker evaluated by offline recall and one live rank (arXiv 2607.23696), an agent-simulation framework backtested for direction on 50 live tests, and a back-testing engine for ad allocation. None of the three is cited in the paper, and the body's claim that no prior work "evaluates the decision value of choosing which experiments to run from pre-launch information alone" is too strong against the creative ranker, which chooses which creatives to test from pre-launch information and reports recall. The claim survives in a narrower form (no calibrated outcome distribution, no abstention, no proper scoring against live results on public archives, no product experiments). What would fix it: cite the three (fetch-verified, as the project's rule requires), state the narrower claim, and add a row for them to Table 11 rather than folding them into "Simulation and LLM agents".

- [minor] Section 2, credit. Several ideas the paper relies on are used without credit to their origin; all of the following should be fetch-verified before they are added, as the project's own rule requires. Rolling-origin evaluation is a forecasting-literature protocol (Tashman, International Journal of Forecasting, 2000). Leakage as a named failure of evaluation design (Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman, ACM TKDD, 2012) is the frame for Section 6.1 and the audit. The admission guard is a one-sided paired test of predictive accuracy with clustered errors, which is the Diebold and Mariano (1995) and Giacomini and White (2006) line, and naming it would let the authors cite its known small-sample behaviour instead of measuring the selection bias only indirectly. The ratio-metric coupling in Section 6.1 is the delta-method ratio-metric problem that Deng, Knoblich and Lu (KDD 2018) treat for in-test analysis; the paper's contribution is to show what it does to a pre-launch forecast, and that is clearer when the in-test treatment is cited. The oracle's value definition (best positive observed lift among a test's contrasts) is inflated by the winner's curse and by the number of arms, which Lee and Shen (KDD 2018) quantify for online experiments. The claim that P1 "is what a power calculation and a test-and-roll budget need" is the assurance idea (O'Hagan, Stevens and Campbell, Pharmaceutical Statistics, 2005; the landscape's section 5.6 already lists it). On the base-rate-as-prior credit the lead asked about: the paper writes that the program-wide success rate used as a prior "originates with" Kohavi, Deng and Vermeer [36]; the authors' own note (related-work.md 1.3) records that Deng (2015) [18] learned the prior, including the share of null effects, from historical experiments seven years earlier, and the base-rate-in-a-false-positive-risk computation that [36] applies descends from Wacholder et al. (2004) and Ioannidis (2005). "Compiled across programs and applied as a prior by [36], following [18]" is the accurate sentence.

- [minor] Section 5 and the whole paper: there are no figures. Section 5 promises reliability diagrams and none is shown; the central methodological finding (the reversed gradient of Section 6.1 against the clean gradient of Section 6.3) is two appendix tables (Tables 12 and 13) that a reader must align by hand. What would fix it: one two-panel figure in the body (mean p_sig and event rate by precision decile, cautionary run beside clean run) and one reliability diagram with consistency bars at the final origin. This is the most important presentation change in the paper.

- [minor] Structure of the nine-page body. Section 4.4 spends most of a column on components the results never exercise (nightly fit, champion and challenger registry, HTTP and tool interfaces, simulation-agent protocol, knowledge-cutoff split); compress it to one paragraph and move the rest to an appendix. The landscape paragraph (Section 2, "Tooling landscape") can go entirely to Appendix C with one sentence left in Related Work. The Discussion repeats Results (calibration without discrimination, the guard share, the weekly replay) and can lose half its length. Use the space for Tables 12, 13 and 14 and the figure above, which carry the claims the abstract makes. The paragraph "The hypotheses that failed" belongs at the start of Results as a pre-registration ledger, not in Limitations; the intro already points there.

- [minor] Abstract ("13,453 admitted contrasts from 4,873 headline tests"). The 4,873 is the number of tests in the exploratory split; the admitted contrasts come from 3,870 tests (count on the ledger snapshot: 13,453 admitted rows, 3,870 distinct test_id), and 3,797 of those are scored. Write "from 3,870 of the split's 4,873 tests".

- [minor] Section 10 and the "every number is reproduced by one command" claim (abstract, Section 1). Four numbers carry PENDING source comments (the released-row scores of the trigger ablation, derived by hand from two aggregate rows; the guard admission rates 39 and 8 percent; the impressions correlation; the top-1,000 tail shares), and the cross-platform agreement "to about one part in 10^11" is sourced to a continuous-integration run with no report JSON. The lead flagged the first set as in progress; the second needs an artifact (commit the CI log or a small report) or a softer sentence. Until both land, the claim should read "every number in the tables".

- [minor] Section 9 (Ethics), what a launching team might misuse. Three uses are not addressed. First, the feedback loop: a team that prioritizes by p_win writes a ledger selected on the forecaster's own output, and the next nightly fit learns from that selected ledger; the Clark (2026) citation is the hook, and the mitigation is cheap (record on each ledger row whether a card influenced the decision to run it, and report skill on card-prioritized and other tests separately). Second, anchoring of the P3 team forecast: Section 4.2 freezes P3 "at setup" but does not say it must be entered before the card is seen. Third, an abstained card "still shows the base rate", which a team will read as the forecast; the card should label it as the quantity the card declined to improve on. One short paragraph covers all three.

- [minor] Section 6.2 ("equals the always_reference baseline"). 0.5725 against 0.5785 (tables.aggregate cell=ALL direction_accuracy for P1 and always_reference) is "matches within the interval", not "equals". The creation-order effect itself deserves a sentence: the archive paper may say whether the earliest-created package is the editor's original headline, which would explain the baseline and tell a reader which way the sign convention runs.

- [minor] Writing. Several sentences run past 80 words with four or more semicolons (the weekly-replay sentence in Section 6.6, the simulation-agent sentence in Section 4.4, the first sentence of "Local pooling"). Numbers are stacked in prose where a table already holds them (Sections 6.3, 6.5, 7). The abstract is about 330 words, above the limit of every candidate venue. The title promises "Arguing from Precedent" and the result is that precedent never earns a weight; a title that states the finding (the ledger gives a calibrated prior, not a winner) would set the right expectation.

## Questions for the authors
1. Which probability is scored as P0 in Table 1, and why does the base-rate rung have a Brier skill of -0.124 against the base rate? Is the empirical-frequency forecaster scored anywhere as a rung?
2. Within one cell and origin, is P1's p_win identical for every test? If so, what share of each period's budget K was filled by the random tie-break, and what is the decision value of text_lr, local_eb and GBM?
3. What gate sizes does the power simulation give at a base rate of 0.1, and does any cell become undecidable at skill 0.10?
4. How do the main results move if the significance event is defined on the planned s_star rather than the realized standard error?
5. The seven-day horizon is a modelling choice the archive does not record. What do the pooled scores look like at three and fourteen days?
6. Is the creation-order effect behind always_reference (0.5785) documented by the archive, for example as the editor's original headline being the first package? If so, is "variant minus reference" the sign a practitioner would expect?
7. For venue purposes: do the authors regard the protocol and benchmark, or the system, as the primary contribution? The paper is currently written as a system paper whose system is mostly unevaluated.

## What is good
- The cautionary run (Section 6.1) and the calibration-by-precision diagnostic are a crisp, transferable lesson: a ratio metric against a noisy reference scored at realized precision reverses the calibration gradient (decile 1 of realized s: P1 mean p_sig 0.163 against an event rate of 0.005) while the headline score hides it. Keep it as a contribution and give it the figure it deserves.
- Pre-registered hypotheses reported as they came out, with the refuted ones kept and named (Section 9), and a plan that said so before the runs.
- The admission guard with a paired, clustered, out-of-time comparison, and the honest reading of it: it recovers most of the loss (guarded minus unguarded 0.0403 nats, 0.0327 to 0.0481; Brier skill 0.0233, 0.0180 to 0.0281) and none of the gain, because there is no gain on this archive.
- Floors and ceilings beside every score, bootstrap by test because contrasts share a reference arm, decision value kept apart from calibration, and the sentence that a calibrated forecaster with no discrimination has zero decision value.
- The leakage audit as a separate recomputation, snapshot digests, one command per table with the report path printed at the top of each, committed checksums, nothing bundled, and the holdout and confirmatory files refused by name. Archive norms are respected and stated.
- The deployment-shaped archive reported as undecidable rather than hidden, with the nominal calendar flagged every time an ASOS number appears; the synthetic recovery of the generator; the learning curve that is flat from 601 rows and explained.
- The weekly replay as an operational check of the drift rule, with the 2013 stretch of flags matching the one origin where the prior fell behind the base rate.
- A fetch-verified landscape survey and a fetch-verified bibliography, which is rarer than it should be.
- The boundary sentence, stated once and kept: the card prioritizes and sizes, the live test decides.

## Venue
Send it to the NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks track, reframed as a benchmark paper: the rolling-origin protocol with its visibility rule, planned-exposure scale and gates; the two public archives with their admission rules, checksums and norms; the ladder and the three challengers as baselines on matched splits; the cautionary run as a benchmark-design lesson; and the released report card as the way an organization runs the same benchmark privately. That track rewards exactly what this paper does well (a reproducible protocol, honest negative baselines, a leakage finding, documented data norms) and does not penalize a negative headline result or an unevaluated serving layer. The KDD applied data science track is a weak fit: it expects a deployment and measured impact, and the paper has neither, only a protocol and a null on one publisher's headline tests. The Web Conference is possible, since the main archive is web content and the text-based winner-prediction line is its literature, but its reviewers would judge the method on impact and the method's result is negative. CODE@MIT is the right audience for the talk and the leakage finding, and the authors should present there, but it is not an archival venue for a paper of this size. Whichever venue, the blocking finding and the four majors above must be resolved first: the baseline the skill numbers are measured against must be the one the text names, the decision-value null must be stated as structural for feature-free rungs and measured for the rungs that can rank, and the abstract must say what the limitations section says.
