## Summary (five sentences, in the reviewer's own words, of what the paper claims and shows)
The paper asks whether a ledger of concluded A/B tests carries out-of-time skill about the next test, and answers on the Upworthy exploratory split with a rolling-origin backtest of a three-rung ladder (cell base rate, pooled Student-t empirical-Bayes prior, guarded kernel-weighted precedents) scored with proper scores, floors, a calibration null, coverage, power-derived gates and a captured-lift decision measure. It reports that the prior improves the log score over the base rate by 0.145 nats and covers near nominal rates, but has Brier skill 0.0015 (interval minus 0.0018 to 0.0048) against the cell base rate, no direction skill beyond the creation-order effect, and no decision value over random ordering, while every learned challenger and every setting of the precedent rung scores at or below the prior. It presents a first run on relative lift at realized precision, with Brier skill minus 0.107 and a reversed calibration gradient, as a leakage finding that motivates a pre-launch protocol with a planned-exposure standard error and calibration by stratum of precision. The deployment-shaped ASOS archive returns undecidable at every gate and a synthetic archive recovers its parameters. I recomputed the pooled P1 and P0 log scores, their paired by-test difference, the P1 Brier skill with its by-test interval, the coverage and the ECE percentile from the per-row score table and matched the report to four decimals; the problems I found are in what the numbers are compared against, not in the arithmetic.

## Findings

- [blocking] Section 6.3, Table 13, abstract ("covers at nominal rates", "the forecasts are calibrated"), `src/mootcourt/scale.py` and `harness.py:615`. The pooled coverage hides a gradient the paper's own protocol says must not exist. Table 13 stratifies by planned exposure only, but the realized standard error depends on exposure and on the realized click-through level, and the forecaster fixes the level at the cell median. From `scores-upworthy-20261103-4bfb354b87e3.parquet` (rung P1, 13,247 rows) the ratio of realized to planned standard error has a 5th to 95th percentile range of 0.527 to 1.493, and by decile of that ratio the 90 percent coverage runs 1.000, 0.999, 0.994, 0.968, 0.945, 0.918, 0.863, 0.838, 0.766, 0.655 while the event rate runs 0.036 to 0.120 and the mean p_sig stays flat at 0.100 to 0.111. The intervals are therefore wrong in both directions row by row and average to 0.8945 (`tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=P1].coverage_90`). Contribution 2 states that a forecaster calibrated overall but wrong inside a precision stratum is not calibrated; by that standard the P1 coverage claim in the abstract and Section 6.3 is not supported. What would fix it: add calibration by decile of realized-to-planned standard error (an evaluation-time stratification, which the protocol already allows for the event) to every report and to Table 13; treat the pre-launch level as uncertain, so that the predictive for the observed estimate is a scale mixture over the cell's visible reference rates rather than a Normal at one s_star (a one-line change on the grid); re-run E1 and E2; and reword the abstract and Section 6.3 to "covers at nominal rates on average and not within strata of the realized level". The same table shows that the absolute-difference scale still couples the effect with the level (event rate 0.036 in the lowest ratio decile against 0.120 in the highest), a milder form of the coupling the cautionary run describes, which Section 6.1 should say.

- [blocking] Sections 4.2, 6.2 and 7, Table 19, `src/mootcourt/predictors/p2.py:446-447` and `:508-515`. The negative result on the precedent rung is confounded with a change of distribution family. At lambda 0 P2 returns P1's Student-t predictive; at any lambda above zero it returns a Normal whose variance is a convex combination of the moment-matched prior variance and the precedent variance (`v = lam (v_m + tau_res2) + (1 - lam) tau^2` with `tau` the standard-deviation equivalent). Every P1 cell and period in the bundle `models/5f1c07efd730/model.json` has nu 3, so the standard-deviation equivalent is 1.73 times the t scale and the Normal has neither the t core nor its tails. The ablation table shows the size of the confound: P1 with a Normal prior scores minus 0.9572 (`ablations/...--prior-normal.json`, rung P1 logscore) against minus 0.8812 for the t, a cost of 0.076 nats, and P2 at fixed lambda 0.2 scores minus 0.9662 (`--lambda-0.2.json`, rung P2), so of the 0.085 nats P2 loses at lambda 0.2 about 0.076 is the family switch and about 0.009 is attributable to the precedents. The selection and the admission guard compound this: `_choose` scores the lambda 0 baseline as a Normal with the standard-deviation equivalent (`p2.py:446-447`, documented in docs/method.md call 56), so the guard admits candidates that beat a Normal stand-in for P1, and those candidates are then deployed out of time against the real t prior and lose. The sentence "the guard recovers most of the loss and none of the gain, because on this archive the precedents carry no gain to recover" and contribution 3's "ablations over weight, bandwidth, features, selection rule, pooling and prior shape leave unchanged" are not supported, because no ablation holds the family fixed while varying lambda. What would fix it: give P2 a working predictive of the same family as P1 (a t or a scale mixture with the cell's nu, so that lambda to zero is continuous), score the lambda 0 baseline inside the selection with P1's actual predictive, re-run E1 and E6, and report the fixed-lambda rows against P1-normal as the controlled comparison until then. The Brier skill rows (P1-normal minus 0.0158, lambda 0.2 minus 0.0558) suggest the precedents do cost calibration of p_sig beyond the family switch, so the conclusion may survive, but the paper cannot show it with the present runs.

- [major] Section 5 (Gates), Section 6.2, Section 9, Table 20, `src/mootcourt/simulate.py:31,84-87`. The gate sizes are simulated at a base rate of 0.3 with per-row resampling and a 95 percent interval, while the archive's base rate is about 0.1 (`tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=P1].brier_base` 0.0832, event rate 0.0910) and the reported intervals are 90 percent by test. Running `gate_power` at base 0.1 (300 replicates, 300 resamples) gives power 0.66 at n 375 for skill 0.10 and about 0.90 only near n 700, and power 0.70 at n 225 and 0.60 at n 175 for skill 0.20, reaching 0.91 at n 400. Table 20's sizes are therefore about half of what the archive's own base rate needs, and the labels "decidable at skill 0.20 only" for the both cell (225 tests) and the reading of 2015 (401 tests) as above the smaller gate are overstated; the pooled table (3,797 tests) is unaffected. What would fix it: simulate the gates at each cell's own base rate and with the same 90 percent by-test criterion the report uses (or state why the 95 percent per-row criterion is kept), print the base-rate-specific sizes beside Table 20, and say in Section 6.2 that the powered-negative claim rests on the interval (minus 0.0018 to 0.0048), which excludes 0.10 by a wide margin regardless of the gate, not on the gate.

- [major] Section 6.4, Table 14, `src/mootcourt/decision_value.py:44-51`. The decision-value comparison carries no uncertainty and its random baseline is a constant. The expected captured fraction of a uniformly random subset of K of n tests is K/n by linearity, so "random captures 0.250" is the budget fraction restated, and the 200 seeded draws measure nothing. P1's p_win is constant within a cell and quarter, so the p_win policy ranks cells and takes the mean value at the tie, which is random order within a cell; the text should say that the Upworthy decision value of P1 is a between-cell choice. The paper says no interval is computed for the difference from random order; one is needed, since the claim "captures the same share as random" is the abstract's headline on decision value. What would fix it: a by-test (or by-origin) bootstrap interval for captured_fraction(p_win) minus K/n per rung, in Table 14 and in the abstract, and a sentence on what the p_win policy can and cannot rank for a rung whose p_win does not vary within a cell.

- [major] Section 6.3, Section 5 (Calibration), `src/mootcourt/harness_stats.py:118-132`. The perfect-calibration null resamples outcomes independently per row, while the paper's own interval rule says contrasts of one test share a reference arm and must be resampled together. The null is therefore too narrow and the percentile anti-conservative. I checked whether the conclusion survives: in the 0.1 to 0.2 bin (6,508 rows, 1,999 tests) the gap between mean p_sig and frequency is 0.0253 with a by-test 90 percent interval of 0.0172 to 0.0331 and a design effect of 1.36; in the 0 to 0.1 bin the design effect is 1.61 and the gap is zero. The rejection stands; the method does not. Separately, the ECE uses ten equal-width bins of which three are populated (`tables.reliability_bins`), the estimator the paper's own citation of Vaicenavicius warns about; with ten equal-mass bins the ECE is 0.0148 and still above all 500 null draws. What would fix it: draw the null by test (resample tests, then outcomes), report the design effect, use equal-mass bins or a debiased estimator, and state that 500 null draws can resolve at most the 99.8th percentile so "100th percentile" means "above every draw".

- [major] Section 6.6, Table 5, Appendix D, `src/mootcourt/loop.py:95-101` and `:129-131`, `scripts/replay_weekly.py`. The weekly replay scores every card as a Normal rebuilt from the frozen point and 90 percent interval (`_forecast_logscore`: sd equal to the interval width over 2 times 1.645), and the docstring concedes that a card that fell back to P1's t prior is scored by that approximation. With nu 3 the rebuilt Normal has standard deviation 1.43 times the t scale against 1.73 for the moment-matched Normal and has no tails, so the replay's model log score is handicapped relative to the backtest by an amount of the order of the 0.076 nats the t prior gains over a Normal (ablation `--prior-normal`), while the drift margin is 0.10 nats and P0 is scored exactly. The 49 flagged weeks, against zero drift flags in the quarterly backtest after burn-in, are partly this handicap: the 2014 and 2015 flags of the both cell (`replay-upworthy-...json` weeks[].reliability.both) rest on 6 to 37 outcomes with rolling log scores 0.13 to 1.0 nats below P0. The 2013Q3 cluster is probably real, since the quarterly table also has P1 below P0 at the July 2013 origin (`tables.score_by_origin`: minus 1.7272 against minus 1.5495). What would fix it: store the predictive family and parameters (nu, scale, grid digest) on the card and score the outcome with the card's own predictive; re-run the replay; and report the replay's coverage (0.860) and the backtest's (0.8945) as different numbers for a stated reason.

- [major] Sections 6.2, 6.6, 7 and 9. The paper reads more than forty unadjusted 90 percent intervals (three cells by three rungs, three years by three rungs, nine origins, five learning-curve fractions by five rungs, four ASOS metrics, the ablation rows) and singles out the ones that clear zero: "the one cell where it clears zero" (image_only, 0.0025 to 0.0154) and "the one year in which the prior clears zero" (2015, 0.0157 to 0.0477). Two excursions among forty intervals at 90 percent is what the null predicts. What would fix it: state the number of intervals read, label the per-cell and per-year intervals as descriptive, and either adjust the family (a simple Bonferroni over the three cells would need 96.7 percent intervals) or stop drawing attention to the excursions; the pooled interval is the pre-registered claim and it is enough.

- [minor] Section 4.2 ("P0 is the baseline every skill number is measured against") against Table 1 (P0 Brier skill minus 0.1237, ECE 0.1016 at the 100th percentile). The Brier skill baseline is the cell's empirical significant-positive frequency (docs/method.md call 1, `harness.py:622`), not the P0 rung's p_sig, which pushes a Normal that ignores measurement error through the significance rule and is badly calibrated. Say in Section 4.2 and in the Table 1 caption that the base rate used for skill is the frequency and that the P0 row's p_sig is a different, deliberately naive forecaster.

- [minor] Section 6.1 ("a calibrated forecaster with the base rate as its floor cannot have negative skill against it") against Section 6.2 (P1 on headline_only: Brier skill minus 0.0043, interval minus 0.0085 to minus 0.0006, `tables.aggregate[cell=headline_only,rung=P1]`). By the paper's own sentence this interval says P1 is miscalibrated on the largest cell, which the ECE row for that cell (0.0172, percentile 100) agrees with, and the text does not connect the two. Qualify the sentence (it holds for a forecaster whose probabilities average to the same base rate and are calibrated) and read the headline_only interval as what it is.

- [minor] Section 6.7. "Undecidable" is a statement about power for a positive skill. P1's ASOS interval (minus 0.2366 to minus 0.0020) excludes zero on the negative side and its ECE is 0.1271 at the 100th percentile with mean p_sig 0.178 against an event rate of 0.139 (`backtest-asos-...json`, `scores-asos-...parquet`), so the archive does resolve something: the prior is overconfident there. Say so beside the undecidable label.

- [minor] Table 1 and Section 5 ("floors and ceilings are reported beside every score"). Table 1 has no Brier and no Brier floor column; the JSON has brier 0.0830, brier_base 0.0832 and brier_floor 0.0553 for P1. Add both columns, and say what the floor means here: it integrates the posterior given the row's own estimate, so it is an in-hindsight bound, and the 0.028 between P1 and the floor is not available to a pre-launch forecaster.

- [minor] Section 6.5 and Section 8 ("decision instability never fires, because the bootstrap over precedents is stable when the precedent weight is zero"). `abstain.py:182-183` returns without resampling whenever lambda is zero, so the trigger is switched off by construction in the 25 of 27 cell-origin pairs where the guard held lambda at zero; the sentence should say "by construction" rather than present it as an empirical observation.

- [minor] Abstract and Section 8 ("the forecasts are calibrated"). Three different things are measured: pooled interval coverage (near nominal), calibration of p_sig against the null (rejected, small and one-sided), and coverage within strata of the realized level (the blocking finding above). Use one word for each.

- [minor] Section 5 (Gates) and Section 6. The paper uses three interval conventions without saying so: 90 percent by test for every reported score, a per-row null for the ECE percentile, and a 95 percent per-row interval inside the gate simulation. State all three in Section 5.

- [nit] Section 6.2 ("equals the always_reference baseline"). P1's direction accuracy is 0.5725 against 0.5785, so it is 0.006 below the baseline, not equal; "is no better than" is accurate.

- [nit] Section 5 (Scores) and `harness.py:496-525`. The Brier floor and the direction ceiling replace the t prior by a Normal with the standard-deviation equivalent before computing the posterior; with nu 3 in every cell this is a material approximation and the text should say so.

- [nit] Section 4.2. Every cell and every period selects nu 3, the smallest value of the grid (3, 5, 10, 30, infinity). A fit that sits on the boundary of its grid is a sign the grid is too narrow; nu below 3 breaks the standard-deviation pooling, which is a reason to report the boundary, not to hide it.

## Questions for the authors
1. Every P1 fit selects nu 3, the boundary of the grid. Did you try 2.5 or 2, and how would the pooling of the scale (which needs nu above 2) be done if the likelihood wants a heavier tail?
2. At the July 2013 origin P1 is 0.18 nats below P0 on 1,273 rows (`tables.score_by_origin`). What happened in that quarter: a scale misfit from the thin 2013Q1 and Q2 history, a burst of large effects, or a horizon artefact?
3. The guard admitted lambda 0.8 in headline_only at 2013-10-01 (in-selection gain 0.0051 nats, se 0.0017, `tables.p2_selection`) where the unguarded tie rule chose 0.5, and P2 then lost 0.078 nats to P1 at that origin. Both of the guard's two admissions on this archive hurt out of time. The synthetic calibration of the multiplier used cells of a few hundred rows; does the false-admission rate hold at 5,000 rows, where a 2-standard-error gain is 0.005 nats, and would forward-chaining validation inside the guard have refused these two?
4. The ratio of realized to planned standard error spans 0.53 to 1.49 between its 5th and 95th percentiles. Is there any pre-launch estimate of a headline test's level better than the cell median, for example the reference headline's own prior tests, and how much of the spread would it remove?
5. On the absolute-difference scale the event rate runs from 0.036 to 0.120 across deciles of realized-to-planned standard error, so the effect still scales with the level. Was a standardized pre-launch scale (the effect divided by s_star) or a logit difference considered, and does the cautionary argument of Section 6.1 apply to it?
6. What is the by-test interval for captured_fraction(p_win) minus K/n at each origin, and does the October 2014 excursion (0.2988 against 0.2508) survive it?

## What is good (so it is not removed in revision)
- The six hypotheses were written before the runs and every one is reported as it came out, including the three refuted; the plan file and Section 9 keep that honest, and the paper gains credibility from it.
- The scoring discipline is right in its parts: strictly proper scores on the same predictive object, bootstrap by test with a stated reason, a Brier skill baseline that is itself learned from history, floors and ceilings beside the scores, calibration by stratum as a required table, and the leakage guard and audit that raise rather than warn.
- The cautionary run (Section 6.1) is a real methodological contribution: the coupling of a ratio effect with its own standard error through the reference arm, shown by decile with numbers, is the kind of thing practitioners get wrong and the paper states it with a reproducible command.
- The admission guard records, per cell and origin, the selected and unguarded lambda, the gain and its clustered standard error (`tables.p2_selection`), so a reviewer can see exactly where and why the rung spoke; keep that table.
- Every number I checked traces to a report JSON path and recomputes from the per-row parquet: P1 logscore minus 0.8812, P0 minus 1.0259, paired difference 0.1447 with a by-test 90 percent interval of 0.1334 to 0.1561 (2,000 resamples), Brier skill 0.0015 with my interval of minus 0.0019 to 0.0052 against the report's minus 0.0018 to 0.0048, coverage 0.8122 and 0.8945, ECE 0.0137 above all 500 null draws. The traceability comments in the LaTeX make this review possible and should stay.
- The decision-value measure is kept apart from calibration and never folded into one number, and the synthetic archive shows what it responds to (between-cell differences) and what it cannot (within-cell), which is the right way to introduce it.
