On one publisher's archive, a base rate with a heavy-tailed density, fitted to concluded experiments, predicts how the next result will be distributed nearly as well as a fitted prior, whose small lead is not shown with the seven scored quarters as the units (Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results} gives the limits of that interval). More history improved the forecast at each scored origin. The prior's skill on which test will be significant is small and fragile, a better level and not a ranking of tests, and the forecasts as issued fail a calibration test. Weighting by precedents does not beat the prior on the exploratory split, where a control shows that the replay could miss a planted signal, and its small lead on the confirmatory split is not clear of zero after adjustment. A simple text classifier ranks tests better than a random order matched on arm count and is not shown to rank them better than counting arms. Abstention is not shown to select better or worse forecasts, and in simulation sizing a test from the forecast shows no measurable advantage over a base rate.

A late addition scores readers played by a language model on which headline of a test drew more clicks (Section~\ref{sec:readers}). In the words fixed beforehand, and with contamination not excluded, a panel of ten simulated readers is not shown to match the observed direction more often than always choosing the headline written first, while aligning the readers with earlier results raised that share and adding them to a classifier fitted on earlier tests raised its share. The first gain is from one method, weights over the readers, in the first of two runs: its adjusted interval ends at 0.0004, inside the margin of 0.0010 that this paper applies elsewhere and that was not part of the readers' design, and in the second run of the same requests the interval contains zero, so we treat that gain as not established. Analyses added afterwards leave this as it is. With the seven quarters as the units that gain is not shown either. An open model, asked directly without readers, is not shown to differ from the rule that picks the headline written first. The gain from adding the primary model's panel to a classifier holds against a stronger classifier on embeddings.

Each number but the three registered verdicts is exploratory, from one split of one publisher's archive read again and again (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). On the confirmatory split, opened once under a registration, each of three readings returned its exploratory verdict: the prior's lead with the quarters as the units and its skill against a smoothed base rate are not shown, and the text classifier is not shown to rank better than counting arms (Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory}). That split covers the same quarters, so tests from a new quarter stand as the next test. A team reading this paper can take away the protocol: apply the same backtest to its own ledger and read that report's intervals. The gates tell how many tests a stated skill requires if the forecast is calibrated and the base rate is known and constant, as the gate simulation assumes, and the abstentions state the condition each forecast met. Here, neither one was shown to pick out the good forecasts. On the single archive big enough to read, the backtest pointed to where forecasts fail, and nobody has yet shown it certifying the forecasts that succeed.
