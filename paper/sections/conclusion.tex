On one publisher's archive, a base rate with a heavy-tailed density, fitted to concluded experiments, predicts how the next result will be distributed nearly as well as a prior fitted to the same experiments. The prior's pooled lead is small. Its interval lies above zero with the seven scored quarters held fixed, and with the quarters as the units it contains zero, so the lead is not shown; seven values are few, and that interval treats them as exchangeable. Without the quarter of July 2014 the pooled interval contains zero as well, and with January 2014 left out the interval with the quarters as the units lies above zero, so the reading turns on single quarters either way. Both forecasters beat a reference that uses no past effect and assumes no test has one. That reference is a floor, and nearly all of the gain over it is already in the base rate. More history improved the forecast: at each of the seven scored origins, a prior fitted on one fourth of the past tests scored worse than one fitted on all of them. The prior's skill on which test will be significant is small and fragile. With tests resampled its interval lies above zero, also without the one cell and quarter whose base rate was zero and on the event at planned precision. With the seven quarters as the units it lies above zero only narrowly, and it is not shown once that cell and quarter is left out, nor on the event at planned precision (Table~\ref{tab:fmt-skill-by-origin}). After recalibration it is not shown even with tests resampled (Table~\ref{tab:fmt-challengers}). It is a better level per cell and quarter and not a ranking of tests, and a large share of it comes from the one cell and quarter in which the unsmoothed base rate was zero. In the main run, forecasts as issued fail a calibration test against the realized event, and on equal-width bins, the binning our rejection rule reads, that rejection does not hold in three ablations (Section~\ref{sec:calibration-results}). Since the prior gives one probability per cell and quarter, the error is one of level, and the level misses in both directions across quarters. On the exploratory split, weighting by precedents does not beat the prior, and a positive control on five simulated ledgers shows that the precedent rung, replayed this way, recovers too little of a planted signal for that result to rule out a signal of the planted size. On the confirmatory split the rung's pooled estimate is a small lead that is not clear of zero after adjustment for the number of intervals read, a registered secondary reading that carries no verdict; a split by cell, not registered and read afterwards, shows opposite signs in the two larger cells (Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory}). A simple text classifier on the headlines ranks tests better than a random order matched on arm count. Against a rule that only counts arms its interval spans zero, on a target that itself grows with the number of arms, and it fails the planted control, which therefore neither validates nor explains that ranking. With one contrast per test, a target without that structure, the classifier ranks tests above a random order on the confirmatory split, a secondary reading without a registered verdict (Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory}). These models are simple stand-ins, so the result sets no bound on what published text models would achieve under this protocol. Abstention is not shown to select better or worse forecasts, and it stays as a label of evidence. In the sizing simulation, at its primary audience size, sizing a test from the forecast shows no measurable advantage over sizing it from a base rate.

A late addition asks whether readers played by a language model can say which headline of a test drew more clicks (Section~\ref{sec:readers}). On the headline-only tests of the exploratory split, with nothing hidden from the model's training and contamination not excluded, a panel of ten simulated readers is not shown to match the observed direction more often than always choosing the earliest arm, the headline written first. For the two smaller of the three models, the panel matched the direction less often than that rule in both runs. Aligning the readers with earlier results raised that share, and adding the simulated readers to a classifier fitted on earlier tests raised its share of directions matched. Those are the words fixed beforehand, and both gains are small. The first gain is from one method, weights over the readers, in the first of two runs of the same requests. The lower end of its adjusted interval is 0.0004, which the margin of 0.0010 that this paper applies elsewhere would not read as clear of zero. That margin was not part of the readers' design. In the second run the interval contains zero, so we treat the gain as not established. For the smallest of the three models, another of the seven methods of alignment scored below the plain panel in both runs, with an unadjusted interval below zero in the second. That comparison was also picked out after the table was seen. The gain to the classifier holds in both runs and still leaves the classifier not above the rule that always chooses the earliest arm. One alignment, a note on the audience that the model writes from earlier results, was more accurate than that rule for the largest of the three models in both runs; it was not among the readings fixed beforehand, we picked it out after seeing all seven methods, and it needs a test of its own.

The claims come with limits. Each number but the three registered verdicts of Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory} is exploratory: a single split of a single publisher's archive, looked at again and again, with the method changed from run to run after results had been seen. The main specification is itself one of those changes. Under the first specification, which read each test's own traffic and scored the base rate with a normal density, the prior's lead looked far larger, and Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results} reports both. With exposure fixed, the comparison of the prior with the base rate does not test what the prior adds when a test's precision is known (Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results}). The system has never been run in production, and this paper shows nothing for product experiments. The archive's confirmatory split, opened once under a registration, returned the exploratory verdict on each of three readings: the prior's lead with the quarters as the units is not shown, its skill against a smoothed base rate is not shown, and the text classifier is not shown to rank better than counting arms (Section~\ref{sec:confirmatory}). With tests as the units the lead and that skill are clear of zero on the confirmatory split, so the first verdict turns on the seven quarters, and the second on the seven quarters and on the smoothing of the base rate: against the unsmoothed base rate the interval with the quarters as the units lies above zero, a reading without a registered verdict. That split covers the same quarters, so tests from a new quarter stand as the next test. A team reading this paper can take away the protocol: apply the same backtest to its own ledger and read that report's intervals. The gates tell how many tests a stated skill requires if the forecast is calibrated and the base rate is known and constant, as the gate simulation assumes, and the abstentions state the condition each forecast met. Here, neither one was shown to pick out the good forecasts. On the single archive big enough to read, the backtest pointed to where forecasts fail, and nobody has yet shown it certifying the forecasts that succeed.
