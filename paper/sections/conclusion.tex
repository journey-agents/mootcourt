\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On one publisher's archive, a prior fitted to concluded experiments predicts how the next result will be distributed better than a base rate that is fitted to the same experiments and scored with a normal density. Most of that gain is the shape of the density: against a base rate that is given heavy tails, the prior's lead is small and still clear of zero. At the one origin where it was measured, more history improved that forecast. On the same evidence the prior's skill on which test will be significant is at most small, and whether its interval clears zero depends on how that event is defined and on how exposure enters the forecast. Weighting by precedents is not shown to improve on the prior's score or skill, and a positive control shows that the precedent rung, replayed this way, recovers too little of a planted signal for that result to rule one out. Precedents rank tests a little better than the prior, an edge that may be traffic and not precedents. A text classifier on the headlines ranks tests a little better than a random order matched on arm count. It fails the planted control, so that ranking is unexplained, and against a rule that only counts arms its interval spans zero. Forecasts as issued fail a calibration test against the realized event, and a shift fitted after the fact to each origin and cell removes most of the error. Abstention is not shown to improve what is issued, and it stays as a label of evidence. In the sizing simulation, at its primary audience size, sizing a test from the forecast shows no measurable advantage over sizing it from a base rate.

These claims have limits. Every number is exploratory: one split of one publisher's archive, read repeatedly, with the method changed between runs after results were seen. The archive of product experiments is too small to decide any claim about skill, so nothing here is shown for product experiments, and the system has never run in production. The confirmatory split is unopened and is the next test. What a team can take from this paper is the protocol: run the same backtest on its own ledger, and let that report, with its gates and its abstentions, say what its forecasts are worth. On the one archive large enough to read, that backtest showed where forecasts fail, and it has not yet been shown to certify forecasts that succeed.
