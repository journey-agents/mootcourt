On one publisher's archive, a base rate with a heavy-tailed density, fitted to concluded experiments, predicts how the next result will be distributed nearly as well as a prior fitted to the same experiments. The prior's pooled lead over that base rate is small. Its interval is above zero with the seven scored quarters held fixed, and with the quarters as the units it contains zero, so the lead is not shown; seven values are few, and that interval treats them as exchangeable. With traffic fixed at a value known at the time, the pooled lead is smaller again and rests on one quarter. Against the base rate as first specified, scored with a normal density, the prior's lead is larger and holds with quarters as units, and most of it is the shape of the density. Both forecasters beat a reference that uses no past effect and assumes no test has one, a floor, and nearly all of that gain is already in the base rate. At the one origin where it was measured, more history improved that forecast, for the base rate as well as for the prior. On the same evidence the prior's skill on which test will be significant is at most small. Whether its interval clears zero depends on four choices: the standard error that defines significance, whether the flagged window is kept, whether traffic is fixed (at a value known at the time the skill is 0.0069, with an interval above zero), and whether the base rate follows the last quarter as the prior does. Weighting by precedents is not shown to improve on the prior's score or skill, and a positive control on one simulated ledger shows that the precedent rung, replayed this way, recovers too little of a planted signal for that result to rule out a signal of the planted size. Precedents rank tests a little better than the prior in a comparison unmatched on arms. A rule that only counts arms captures more at a quarter and a half of the tests, so the edge may be arms or traffic and not precedents. A text classifier on the headlines ranks tests a little better than a random order matched on arm count. Against a rule that only counts arms its interval spans zero, and it fails the planted control, which therefore neither validates nor explains that ranking. Forecasts as issued fail a calibration test against the realized event, and a shift fitted after the fact to each origin and cell removes most of the error; how often that test rejects a calibrated forecaster was not measured. Abstention is not shown to improve what is issued, and it stays as a label of evidence. In the sizing simulation, at its primary audience size, sizing a test from the forecast shows no measurable advantage over sizing it from a base rate.

These claims have limits. Every number is exploratory: one split of one publisher's archive, read repeatedly, with the method changed between runs after results were seen. The archive of product experiments is too small to decide any claim about skill, so nothing here is shown for product experiments, and the system has never run in production. The confirmatory split is unopened and is the next test. What a team can take from this paper is the protocol: run the same backtest on its own ledger and read the intervals of that report. The gates say how many tests a stated skill needs, and the abstentions name the condition a forecast met. Neither was shown here to mark which forecasts are good. On the one archive large enough to read, that backtest showed where forecasts fail, and it has not yet been shown to certify forecasts that succeed.
