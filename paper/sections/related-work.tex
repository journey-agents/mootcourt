\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Parts of the system are related to earlier work in this section. Those parts are defined in Sections~\ref{sec:setting} to~\ref{sec:protocol}. A reader new to the system may wish to read them first.

\paragraph{Empirical Bayes over many experiments.}
The fitted prior (P1) and the precedents (P2) rest on shrinking parallel estimates toward a common prior \cite{efron1975stein}, on random-effects meta-analysis \cite{dersimonian1986meta} and on partial pooling \cite{gelman2007multilevel}. In online experimentation, the prior has been learned from thousands of historical tests \cite{deng2015objective}, used to separate true effects from lucky draws \cite{azevedo2019empirical}, argued to be heavy-tailed \cite{azevedo2020fat}, and fit nonparametrically \cite{guo2020empirical}. It has been used to shrink effect estimates by splitting each experiment, which its authors report would improve launch decisions \cite{coey2019improving}, and it has been combined with an experiment-specific shrinkage factor against the winner's curse \cite{mudd2026breaking}. Estimates are distorted by selecting on significance \cite{deng2021postselection}, and a historical prior loses calibration when the corpus is winner-selected or pooled across programs \cite{schultzberg2026bayesian}. Our admission rules read traffic and content, and on the headline archive they also read a minimum number of clicks in each arm. Because clicks are an outcome, the archive is also scored in Section~\ref{sec:ablations} without that rule. We fit our priors per archive and cell. Neither choice is tested in this paper against those failures. Across programs, published success rates have been compiled \cite{kohavi2022intuition,deng2015objective}; the base rate (P0) computes one per cell and scores it, and skill against it follows the forecasting convention of skill against climatology \cite{murphy1973vector}. These models take the standard error of each row as given, and the delta method handles a ratio metric within one test \cite{deng2018delta}. Neither of them covers what our cautionary run shows, which is that the mean relative lift climbs, across tests, with its realized standard error (Section~\ref{sec:cautionary}). A related coupling is known in meta-analysis: for an odds ratio, the estimate and its standard error are correlated \cite{peters2006comparison}.

\paragraph{Forecasting before or during a test, and screening before it.}
Surrogate indices read a test that is already running: they are fitted on earlier data and applied to a test's early outcomes \cite{athey2019surrogate}, and they have been validated as a decision rule \cite{zhang2023evaluating}. Always-valid inference \cite{johari2015always,johari2022always,howard2021timeuniform}, Bayesian optional stopping \cite{deng2016continuous} and learned variance reduction \cite{guo2021machine} do the same. The card we issue comes before the test starts. Before a live test, three recent industrial systems screen candidates:
\begin{itemize}
% src: cited paper lee2026offline: three live tests of 50, 13 and 32 arms
\item an archive-trained ranker of ad creatives for many-armed tests, validated by offline recall and by three live tests of 50, 13 and 32 arms \cite{lee2026offline};
\item a ranker of variants used with customers, trained on the public Upworthy archive and evaluated offline against customer experiments \cite{hu2026experimentation};
\item a back-testing engine, described in a blog post, that runs historical campaign data through a daily process allocating advertising budget \cite{mazzanti2026backtesting}.
\end{itemize}
None of them scores a forecast distribution against the recorded results of the tests it forecast, and none abstains. The first of them reports live tests, which this paper lacks. The closest text baselines are winner predictors on the Upworthy archive (Table~\ref{tab:fmt-prior-work}): marginally better than chance at naming the winner \cite{ye2025lola}, about twice random on decisive tests once retrained on revealed outcomes \cite{clark2026predict}, and above chance in domain, at chance out of it \cite{khimani2026finetuning}.
For that family, our text classifier is a simple stand-in. It is a TF-IDF logistic regression, not a re-implementation of any published model. Its scoring uses the same origins and rows as the ladder.
% src: $RUN6/backtest-upworthy-20261106-2dc461f52ea4.json tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=text_lr].direction_accuracy_decisive 0.5886; tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=always_reference].direction_accuracy_decisive 0.6614, n_decisive 2696
On the decisive rows, the rows where the result is significant either way, it names the direction correctly on 0.589 of them (Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results}). On the same rows, a rule that always picks the reference arm is right on 0.661, so the classifier falls short of that rule on accuracy. We did not rerun the published models. Gradient boosting \cite{friedman2001greedy} uses its scikit-learn implementation \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, and so does TF-IDF term weighting \cite{salton1988term} with logistic regression. The local comparison set takes the shape of \cite{li2025learning}: a prior fitted on a test's nearest past rows, with the neighbour count fixed by hand and with no shrinkage toward the cell prior.

\paragraph{Language-model simulators.}
Simulators that stand in for the audience report only direction \cite{li2026simgym,hut2026can}. Outcomes generated by large language models (LLMs) recover human effects only once calibrated against human data \cite{persson2026statistical,ankargren2026when,ashokkumar2026large}, models prompted with personas drift \cite{bisbee2024synthetic}, and respondent grounding has shown fidelity on self-report, which is not evidence about live experiments \cite{park2024llm}. The optional language-model forecast in the package therefore enters at weight zero.
% src: cited paper schultzberg2026when: 183 Upworthy interventions
Applied to 183 Upworthy interventions, a trial-level surrogacy gate found that a live test was still needed for every one \cite{schultzberg2026when}.

\paragraph{Human forecasts, win rates and decision value.}
Expert forecasts of treatment effects help when they are averaged \cite{dellavigna2018predicting}, should be recorded before results are known \cite{dellavigna2019predict}, and in a megastudy failed to pick the best intervention \cite{milkman2021megastudies}. The team's own forecast, which is a part of the design with no result here, is therefore frozen with a timestamp before the card is seen. No public archive holds such forecasts, so this paper does not score it. Published success rates range from about a third to about a tenth of ideas \cite{kohavi2022intuition,kohavi2009controlled,kohavi2013online,larsen2022statistical}, and a share of significant results is estimated to be nulls \cite{berman2022false}, an estimate that descends from the false-positive report probability \cite{wacholder2004assessing} and the low-prior regime \cite{ioannidis2005why}; the base rate (P0) measures these rates per cell. The launch decision has been cast as a Bayes decision \cite{goldberg2017decision}, and test-and-roll sizes tests from the cost of exposure and rollout \cite{feit2019test}, which is the frame of our sizing simulation (Section~\ref{sec:sizing-results}). Single tests are often underpowered \cite{lewis2015unfavorable}, a program should test more ideas at smaller allocations \cite{sudijono2024optimizing}, and surprising results are usually wrong \cite{kohavi2020trustworthy}. Our novelty trigger labels a forecast for a test whose features are unlike the cell's history. The card's significance probability is an assurance in the sense of \cite{ohagan2005assurance}: it is a success probability averaged over the prior, not a power at one assumed effect. The nearest decision-value evaluation scores launch rules according to the returns they would have produced on historical tests \cite{chou2025evaluating}. Those rules read each experiment's own data, and ours read only the ledger. Our oracle is the best positive observed lift among a test's arms; it carries the winner's curse \cite{lee2018winners} and is an inflated upper bound.

\paragraph{Scoring, calibration, abstention and the protocol.}
The logarithmic score (log score), the Brier score and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) are strictly proper \cite{gneiting2007strictly,matheson1976scoring}, and forecasts should maximize sharpness subject to calibration \cite{gneiting2007probabilistic}. The rolling origin is the out-of-sample protocol of the forecasting literature \cite{tashman2000outofsample}, applied here to versioned snapshots of the ledger, and leakage is used in the sense of \cite{kaufman2012leakage}. Only when a paired comparison of log scores, clustered by test, shows a gain does the admission guard give the precedents weight. That comparison follows tests of equal predictive accuracy \cite{diebold1995comparing,giacomini2006tests}, but we use it as a threshold and not as a formal test. The clustered null and the equal-mass estimator of the expected calibration error (ECE) are motivated by consistency bars for reliability diagrams \cite{brocker2007increasing} and by the pitfalls of binned calibration estimators \cite{vaicenavicius2019evaluating}. Abstention descends from Chow's reject rule \cite{chow1970optimum} and from selective classification \cite{geifman2017selective}. A recent multimodal judge of winners raises its agreement on real tests only behind a vote-margin filter, and that filter abstains on half of them \cite{dooskin2026judge}. Our four triggers (support, novelty, reliability and decision instability) began as a filter of that kind and now only label a forecast's evidence (Section~\ref{sec:abstention-results}). We check interval coverage empirically. A conformal method would guarantee it \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021gentle}, but that guarantee needs exchangeability, and tests across cells and periods do not have it.

\paragraph{The two archives, local pooling, and what appears new.}
Headline and image tests of the publisher are recorded in the Upworthy Research Archive. They run from January 2013 to April 2015 and come in exploratory, confirmatory and hold-out splits \cite{matias2021upworthy}.
% src: $RUN6/paper-extras-upworthy-20261106-2dc461f52ea4.json randomization_window.notice (25 June 2013 to 10 January 2014)
Randomization problems in tests from 25 June 2013 to 10 January 2014 were later flagged by its maintainers. Our main analysis leaves those tests out (Section~\ref{sec:setting}; \cite{upworthy2024notice}). Its concluded tests are analysed in other studies of the archive \cite{banerjee2025language,robertson2023negativity,ottaviani2024testing}.
% src: $RUN6/backtest-asos-20261103-6bb5a15c5ff0.json header.tests_admitted 78, header.tests_scored 68
The ASOS dataset releases daily checkpoints from 78 real experiments, and we score 68 of them \cite{liu2021datasets,liu2024statistical}. Its documented uses \cite{che2024optimization,kim2026odds} concern sequential testing and bandits. Local empirical Bayes is the closest prior art to the precedents (P2) \cite{li2025learning}, and the estimator analogues are Stein-type shrinkage with an auxiliary estimate \cite{rosenman2020combining} and crossed random effects \cite{baayen2008mixed}. The claim that survives the survey is a narrow one. We found no prior system that issues a pre-launch outcome distribution with a win probability for a single proposed experiment, that abstains under named triggers, and that scores it with proper scores, gates and a leakage audit against the results of concluded tests on public archives. On those same rows, the nearest neighbours \cite{li2025learning,ye2025lola,clark2026predict,khimani2026finetuning} are represented by simple stand-ins (a local comparison set and a text classifier) and not by the published systems, so what we report about them speaks only for those stand-ins.
