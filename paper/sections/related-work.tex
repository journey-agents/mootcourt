\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
This section relates parts of the system to earlier work. Sections~\ref{sec:setting} to~\ref{sec:protocol} define those parts, and a reader new to the system may wish to read them first.

\paragraph{Empirical Bayes over many experiments.}
Shrinking parallel estimates toward a common prior \cite{efron1975stein}, random-effects meta-analysis \cite{dersimonian1986meta} and partial pooling \cite{gelman2007multilevel} underlie the fitted prior (P1) and the precedents (P2). In online experimentation the prior has been learned from thousands of historical tests \cite{deng2015objective}, used to separate true effects from lucky draws \cite{azevedo2019empirical}, argued to be heavy-tailed \cite{azevedo2020fat}, fit nonparametrically \cite{guo2020empirical}, used to shrink effect estimates by splitting each experiment, which its authors report would improve launch decisions \cite{coey2019improving}, and combined with an experiment-specific shrinkage factor against the winner's curse \cite{mudd2026breaking}. Selecting on significance distorts estimates \cite{deng2021postselection}, and a historical prior loses calibration when the corpus is winner-selected or pooled across programs \cite{schultzberg2026bayesian}. Our admission rules read traffic and content and, on the headline archive, a minimum number of clicks in each arm. Clicks are an outcome, so Section~\ref{sec:ablations} also scores the archive without that rule. Our priors are fit per archive and cell. This paper does not test either choice against those failures. Published success rates have been compiled across programs \cite{kohavi2022intuition,deng2015objective}; the base rate (P0) computes one per cell and scores it, and skill against it follows the forecasting convention of skill against climatology \cite{murphy1973vector}. These models take each row's standard error as given, and the delta method handles a ratio metric within one test \cite{deng2018delta}. Neither covers what our cautionary run shows: across tests, the mean relative lift climbs with its realized standard error (Section~\ref{sec:cautionary}). Meta-analysis knows a related coupling: for an odds ratio, the estimate and its standard error are correlated \cite{peters2006comparison}.

\paragraph{Forecasting before or during a test, and screening before it.}
Surrogate indices \cite{athey2019surrogate}, validated as a decision rule \cite{zhang2023evaluating}, always-valid inference \cite{johari2015always,johari2022always,howard2021timeuniform}, Bayesian optional stopping \cite{deng2016continuous} and learned variance reduction \cite{guo2021machine} sharpen a running test from its own data; our card is issued before the test starts. Three recent industrial systems screen candidates before a live test:
\begin{itemize}
% src: cited paper lee2026offline: three live tests of 50, 13 and 32 arms
\item an archive-trained ranker of ad creatives for many-armed tests, validated by offline recall and three live tests of 50, 13 and 32 arms \cite{lee2026offline};
\item a ranker of variants used with customers, trained on the public Upworthy archive and evaluated offline on customer experiments \cite{hu2026experimentation};
\item a back-testing engine that replays historical campaign data through its daily allocation process \cite{mazzanti2026backtesting}.
\end{itemize}
None scores a forecast distribution against the recorded results of the tests it forecast, and none abstains. The first of them reports live tests, which this paper does not have. Winner predictors on the Upworthy archive are the closest text baselines (Table~\ref{tab:fmt-prior-work}): marginally better than chance at naming the winner \cite{ye2025lola}, about twice random on decisive tests after retraining on revealed outcomes \cite{clark2026predict}, and above chance in domain, chance out of it \cite{khimani2026finetuning}.
Our text classifier re-implements the simplest member of that family and is scored on the same origins and rows as the ladder.
% src: $RUN6/backtest-upworthy-20261106-2dc461f52ea4.json tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=text_lr].direction_accuracy_decisive 0.5886; tables.aggregate[cell=ALL,rung=always_reference].direction_accuracy_decisive 0.6614, n_decisive 2696
On the decisive rows, where the result is significant either way, it names the direction correctly on 0.589 of them (Section~\ref{sec:ladder-results}). A rule that always picks the reference arm is right on 0.661 of the same rows, so the classifier falls short of that rule on accuracy. The published models were not rerun. The three challengers are the simplest members of their families. Gradient boosting \cite{friedman2001greedy} and TF-IDF term weighting \cite{salton1988term} with logistic regression use their scikit-learn implementations \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. The local comparison set after \cite{li2025learning} is our own implementation.

\paragraph{Language-model simulators.}
Simulators that stand in for the audience report direction only \cite{li2026simgym,hut2026can}; outcomes generated by large language models (LLMs) recover human effects only after calibration against human data \cite{persson2026statistical,ankargren2026when,ashokkumar2026large}, persona-prompted models drift \cite{bisbee2024synthetic}, and respondent grounding buys fidelity on self-report, not on live experiments \cite{park2024llm}. The package's optional language-model forecast therefore enters at weight zero.
% src: cited paper schultzberg2026when: 183 Upworthy interventions
A trial-level surrogacy gate applied to 183 Upworthy interventions found that every one still needed a live test \cite{schultzberg2026when}.

\paragraph{Human forecasts, win rates and decision value.}
Expert forecasts of treatment effects help when averaged \cite{dellavigna2018predicting}, should be recorded before results are known \cite{dellavigna2019predict}, and failed to pick the best intervention in a megastudy \cite{milkman2021megastudies}. The team's own forecast, a part of the design with no result here, is therefore frozen with a timestamp before the card is seen; no public archive holds such forecasts, so this paper does not score it. Published success rates run from about a third to about a tenth of ideas \cite{kohavi2022intuition,kohavi2009controlled,kohavi2013online,larsen2022statistical}, and a share of significant results is estimated to be nulls \cite{berman2022false}, descending from the false-positive report probability \cite{wacholder2004assessing} and the low-prior regime \cite{ioannidis2005why}; the base rate (P0) measures these rates per cell. The launch decision has been cast as a Bayes decision \cite{goldberg2017decision}, and test-and-roll sizes tests from the cost of exposure and rollout \cite{feit2019test}, the frame of our sizing simulation (Section~\ref{sec:sizing-results}). Single tests are often underpowered \cite{lewis2015unfavorable}, a program should test more ideas at smaller allocations \cite{sudijono2024optimizing}, and surprising results are usually wrong \cite{kohavi2020trustworthy}; our novelty trigger labels a forecast for a test whose features are unlike the cell's history. The card's significance probability is an assurance in the sense of \cite{ohagan2005assurance}, a success probability averaged over the prior rather than a power at one assumed effect. The nearest decision-value evaluation scores launch rules by the returns they would have produced on historical tests \cite{chou2025evaluating}; those rules read each experiment's own data, ours only the ledger, and our oracle, the best positive observed lift among a test's arms, carries the winner's curse \cite{lee2018winners} and is an inflated upper bound.

\paragraph{Scoring, calibration, abstention and the protocol.}
The logarithmic score (log score), Brier score and continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) are strictly proper \cite{gneiting2007strictly,matheson1976scoring}, and forecasts should maximize sharpness subject to calibration \cite{gneiting2007probabilistic}. The rolling origin is the forecasting literature's out-of-sample protocol \cite{tashman2000outofsample}, applied here to versioned snapshots of the ledger, and leakage is used in the sense of \cite{kaufman2012leakage}. The admission guard gives the precedents weight only when a paired comparison of log scores, clustered by test, shows a gain. That comparison follows tests of equal predictive accuracy \cite{diebold1995comparing,giacomini2006tests}, but we use it as a threshold, not as a formal test. Consistency bars for reliability diagrams \cite{brocker2007increasing} and the pitfalls of binned calibration estimators \cite{vaicenavicius2019evaluating} motivate the clustered null and the equal-mass estimator of the expected calibration error (ECE). Abstention descends from Chow's reject rule \cite{chow1970optimum} and selective classification \cite{geifman2017selective}. A recent multimodal judge of winners raises its agreement on real tests only behind a vote-margin filter that abstains on half of them \cite{dooskin2026judge}. Our four triggers (support, novelty, reliability and decision instability) began as such a filter; because issued rows were not shown to score better on skill, they now label a forecast's evidence instead. We check interval coverage empirically. A conformal method would guarantee it \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021gentle}, but that guarantee needs exchangeability, which tests across cells and periods do not have.

\paragraph{The two archives, local pooling, and what appears new.}
The Upworthy Research Archive records the publisher's headline and image tests from January 2013 to April 2015 in exploratory, confirmatory and hold-out splits \cite{matias2021upworthy}.
% src: $RUN6/paper-extras-upworthy-20261106-2dc461f52ea4.json randomization_window.notice (25 June 2013 to 10 January 2014)
Its maintainers later flagged randomization problems in tests from 25 June 2013 to 10 January 2014, which our main analysis leaves out (Section~\ref{sec:setting}; \cite{upworthy2024notice}). Prior analyses explain its effects after the fact \cite{banerjee2025language,robertson2023negativity,ottaviani2024testing}.
% src: $RUN6/backtest-asos-20261103-6bb5a15c5ff0.json header.tests_admitted 78, header.tests_scored 68
The ASOS dataset releases daily checkpoints from 78 real experiments, 68 of which we score \cite{liu2021datasets,liu2024statistical}; its documented uses \cite{che2024optimization,kim2026odds} concern sequential testing and bandits. The closest prior art to the precedents (P2) is local empirical Bayes \cite{li2025learning}; Stein-type shrinkage with an auxiliary estimate \cite{rosenman2020combining} and crossed random effects \cite{baayen2008mixed} are the estimator analogues. The claim that survives the survey is narrow: we found no prior system that issues a pre-launch outcome distribution with a win probability for a single proposed experiment, abstains under named triggers, and scores it with proper scores, gates and a leakage audit against the results of concluded tests on public archives. The nearest neighbours \cite{li2025learning,ye2025lola,clark2026predict,khimani2026finetuning} are represented on those same rows by re-implementations of their simplest members (a local comparison set and a text classifier), not by the published systems, so what we report about them speaks for those re-implementations only.
