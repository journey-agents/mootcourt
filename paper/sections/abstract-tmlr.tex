A team wants to know, before launching a change, what its experiment will show. Systems that screen candidates before a live test are evaluated on private data, and public work scores models that name the winning variant. We found no public evaluation that scores a forecast distribution and a probability of a significant result against concluded tests, under a fixed rule for what the forecast may see. We replay one public archive of an online publisher in time order: 2,949 headline and image tests, scored over seven calendar quarters by models fitted only on earlier tests. The archive records the traffic a test received and not the traffic planned, so each forecast is given the median traffic of earlier tests of its kind. A base rate with a heavy-tailed density forecasts the distribution of the next result nearly as well as a prior fitted to past tests: the prior leads by 0.007 nats (units of log density) per comparison, and when the seven quarters, not the tests, are taken as the units, that lead cannot be told apart from zero; because every test of one kind and quarter is given the same traffic, the two are densities fitted to the same past results and differ little by construction, so the comparison does not test what the prior would add if each test's planned traffic were known. The prior's skill at saying which test will be significant is small and fragile. Within a kind of test and quarter it gives every test the same probability, so the skill is a better level and not a ranking of tests. With the quarters as the units, its interval excludes zero only barely once one kind of test in one quarter, where the base rate was zero, is left out, and we do not read that as a skill that is shown. In the main run its probabilities fail a calibration test: the level per kind of test and quarter is off, in both directions, and on the bins that the rule reads the rejection does not hold in three ablations. None of the models we ran, all of them simple stand-ins and not the published predictors, ranks tests detectably better than a rule that counts a test's arms, on a target that itself grows with the number of arms. Weighting similar past tests does not beat the prior; on synthetic ledgers with a planted signal, replayed the same way, at each of five seeds it recovers under a tenth of the gain open to a model that is told the signal, so that tie does not rule out a signal of the planted size. A separate early configuration, on a relative scale, that handed the forecast each test's realized standard error pushed the prior's ranking below chance, and we report it as a caution. Every result but the following is exploratory, because we read one split many times and changed the main specification after seeing results. We then opened the archive's confirmatory split once, with three readings registered beforehand, and on 13,822 further tests of the same quarters each agrees with the exploratory split: the prior's lead with the quarters as the units is not shown, its skill against a smoothed base rate is not shown, and the text classifier is not shown to rank better than counting arms. We release the protocol with an open-source package that regenerates every results table and figure from its reports.
