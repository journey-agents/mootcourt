Before launching a change, a team wants to know what result its experiment will show. Industrial systems screen candidates before live tests and are evaluated on private data, and we found no public evaluation that shows whether forecasts from past experiments can be trusted, or when those experiments say too little. We replay two public archives in time order, so that each forecast is fitted only on tests that ended before its own test began. One archive holds 2,949 scored headline and image tests from an online publisher, after leaving out a period its maintainers flag for randomization problems. The other holds 68 scored experiments from a fashion retailer and has no dates, so its order is arbitrary. The forecasts use the traffic each test received, which stands in for the planned traffic that neither archive records, and we also report a run with it fixed. All results are exploratory: we reused the data and changed the method after seeing results. On the publisher's archive, a prior distribution fitted to earlier tests of the same kind predicts the distribution of the next result better than a base rate scored with a normal density, by 0.120 nats per comparison on the log score. Most of that gain is the shape of the density, which two separate checks show. With both densities normal the gain is 0.045 nats. With both heavy-tailed it is 0.020 nats pooled, and both intervals are above zero. Against the heavy-tailed base rate the prior is not ahead in every quarter, and with the traffic figure fixed as well the gain is 0.009 nats, still above zero. The prior does not say which test will succeed: its skill at forecasting a statistically significant positive result has a 90 percent interval that spans zero under our main definition of that event. Its probabilities fail a calibration test against the event as it turned out. Weighting similar past tests, which an admission check allows in few cases, adds nothing measurable to the prior's score. That tie cannot rule out a real signal: with a known signal planted in one synthetic ledger, the weighting recovers 0.09 of the log score gain of a model that is told the signal, a figure with no interval. A text classifier on the headlines picks tests with more positive lift than a random choice matched on the number of variants. A rule that only counts variants does as well, and the classifier fails the planted-signal control. The retailer's archive is too small to decide any claim about skill. We release the protocol, which adds no new data, and an open-source package that regenerates the results tables and figures from its reports.
