A team wants to know, before launching a change, what its experiment will show. Systems that screen candidates before a live test are evaluated on private data, and public work scores models that name the winning variant. We found no public evaluation that scores a forecast distribution and a probability of a significant result against concluded tests, under a fixed rule for what the forecast may see. We replay one public archive of an online publisher in time order: 2,949 headline and image tests, scored over seven calendar quarters by models fitted only on earlier tests. The archive records the traffic a test received and not the traffic planned, so each forecast is given the median traffic of earlier tests of its kind. A base rate with a heavy-tailed density forecasts the distribution of the next result nearly as well as a prior fitted to past tests: the prior leads by 0.007 nats per comparison on the log score, and with the quarters as the units of resampling that lead is not shown. The prior's skill at saying which test will be significant is small, and within a kind of test and quarter it gives every test the same probability, so the skill is a better level and not a ranking of tests. Its probabilities fail a calibration test: the level per kind of test and quarter is off, on average too high. None of the models we ran, all of them simple stand-ins and not the published predictors, is shown to rank tests better than a rule that counts a test's arms. Weighting similar past tests does not beat the prior; on synthetic ledgers with a planted signal it recovers under a tenth of the gain open to a model told the signal, so that tie does not rule out a signal of the planted size. Every result is exploratory, because we read one split many times and changed the main specification after seeing results; the archive's confirmatory split is unopened. We release the protocol with an open-source package that regenerates every results table and figure from its reports.
