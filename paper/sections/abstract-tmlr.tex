Before launching a change, a team wants to know what result its experiment will show. Industrial systems screen candidates before live tests and are evaluated on private data, and public work on one archive scores models that name the winning variant. We found no public evaluation that scores a forecast distribution and a probability of a significant result against concluded tests, under a fixed rule for what the forecast may see, and that says when the record is too small to judge. We replay two public archives in time order, quarter by quarter, so that each forecast is fitted only on tests that ended before its own test began. One archive holds 2,949 scored headline and image tests from an online publisher, after leaving out a window of dates its maintainers flag for randomization problems. The other holds 68 scored experiments from a fashion retailer and has no dates, so its order is arbitrary. The forecasts use the traffic each test received, which stands in for the planned traffic that neither archive records, and we also report a run with it fixed at a value known at the time. All results are exploratory: we reused the data and changed the method after seeing results. On the publisher's archive, a base rate fitted to earlier tests of the same kind forecasts the distribution of the next result nearly as well as a fitted prior, once both use a heavy-tailed density. Against that base rate the prior's gain is 0.020 nats per comparison on the log score. Its interval is above zero when tests are resampled and the seven scored quarters are held fixed. With the quarters as the units the interval contains zero, so the prior's lead over a heavy-tailed base rate is not shown; seven values are few, and that interval treats them as exchangeable. Scored with a normal density, as first specified, the base rate trails the prior by 0.120 nats, and most of that gap is the shape of the density. With the traffic figure fixed at a value known at the time, the gain over the heavy-tailed base rate is 0.007 nats pooled and rests on one quarter. Both forecasters beat a reference that uses no past effect and assumes no test has one, which is a floor, and nearly all of that gain is already in the base rate. The prior does not say which test will succeed: its skill at forecasting a statistically significant positive result has a 90 percent interval that spans zero under our main definition of that event. Its probabilities fail a calibration test against the realized event, and how often that test rejects a calibrated forecaster was not measured. Weighting similar past tests, which an admission check allows in few cases, adds nothing measurable to the prior's score. That result cannot rule out a real signal: with a known signal planted in one synthetic ledger, the weighting recovers 0.09 of the log score gain of a model that is told the signal, a figure with no interval. A text classifier on the headlines picks tests with more positive lift than a random choice matched on the number of arms. A rule that only counts arms does as well, and the classifier fails the planted-signal control. The retailer's archive is too small to decide any claim about skill. We release the protocol, which adds no new data, and an open-source package that regenerates the results tables and figures from its reports.
