Before launch, a team wants to know what its experiment will show. No public evaluation we found scores a forecast distribution and a probability of significance against concluded tests, under a fixed rule for what it may see. We replay one public archive: 2,949 headline and image tests, scored over seven quarters by models fitted on earlier tests. Given the same traffic, a heavy-tailed base rate forecasts the next result nearly as well as a fitted prior, whose lead of 0.007 nats cannot be told apart from zero with quarters as units. The prior's skill on which test will be significant is small and fragile, and its probabilities fail a calibration test. A text classifier orders tests better than a random order matched on arm count, but no model we ran is shown to beat counting arms. Weighting similar past tests does not beat the prior, in a replay that recovers under a tenth of a planted signal. Readers played by a language model, contamination not excluded, are not shown to name the better headline more often than always picking the earliest; a gain from weighting them is not established. An open model asked directly is not shown to differ from that rule; a classifier on headline embeddings is above it with blocks of tests, not quarters, as units. These findings are exploratory. We opened the archive's confirmatory split once, under rules fixed beforehand in our dated record, held by no outside registry then. On 13,822 tests the three verdicts recur: with quarters as units the prior's lead and its skill against a smoothed base rate are not shown, nor is the classifier shown to rank above counting arms. Two of the three rules read seven quarters. Past tests say what to expect and little about which test to run.
