A team wants to know, before launching a change, what result its experiment will show. Industrial systems screen candidates before live tests, and they are evaluated on private data; public work on one archive scores models that name the winning variant. We found no public evaluation that, under a fixed rule for what the forecast may see, scores a forecast distribution and a probability of a significant result against concluded tests and that says when the record is too small to judge. Quarter by quarter, we replay two public archives in time order, so that each forecast is fitted only on tests that ended before its own test began. One archive, from an online publisher, holds 2,949 scored headline and image tests once a window of dates that its maintainers flag for randomization problems is left out. The other holds 68 scored experiments from a fashion retailer; it has no dates, so its order is arbitrary. The forecasts use the traffic that each test received, which stands in for the planned traffic that neither archive records; we also report a run with it fixed at a value known at the time. All results are exploratory, since we reused the data and changed the method after seeing results. On the publisher's archive, the distribution of the next result is forecast nearly as well by a base rate fitted to earlier tests of the same kind as by a fitted prior, once both use a heavy-tailed density. The prior's gain against that base rate is 0.020 nats per comparison on the log score. When tests are resampled and the seven scored quarters are held fixed, its interval is above zero. With the quarters as the units, the interval contains zero, so the prior's lead over a heavy-tailed base rate is not shown; seven values are few, and that interval treats them as exchangeable. The base rate trails the prior by 0.120 nats when scored with a normal density, as first specified, and most of that gap is the shape of the density. With the traffic figure fixed at a value known at the time, the gain over the heavy-tailed base rate is 0.007 nats pooled, and it rests on one quarter. Both forecasters beat a reference that uses no past effect and assumes no test has one. That reference is a floor, and nearly all of that gain is already in the base rate. The prior does not say which test will succeed: the 90 percent interval of its skill at forecasting a statistically significant positive result spans zero under our main definition of that event. Its probabilities fail a calibration test against the realized event, and we did not measure how often that test rejects a calibrated forecaster. Weighting similar past tests adds nothing measurable to the prior's score, and an admission check allows that weighting in few cases. That result cannot rule out a real signal: in one synthetic ledger with a known signal planted, the weighting recovers 0.09 of the log score gain of a model that is told the signal, and that figure has no interval. A text classifier on the headlines picks tests with more positive lift than a random choice that is matched on the number of arms. A rule that counts only arms does as well, and the classifier fails the planted-signal control. The retailer's archive is too small for deciding any claim about skill. We release the protocol, which adds no new data, together with an open-source package that regenerates the results tables and figures from its reports.
